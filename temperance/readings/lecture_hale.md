## Professor Hale's Lecture

*Background on the temperance movement. As you read, highlight two kinds of things:*

- ***terms you'll meet again in the readings** — like the Second Great Awakening, evangelicals, or the WCTU — and add a note saying what each one means in your own words;*
- ***the reasons Professor Hale gives for why this movement arose when it did** — you'll need these at the end of the module, when you compare the 1800s movement with today's.*

You may be familiar with Dry January or movements today to encourage people to drink less. This is not a new idea, though what you observe today is in some ways very different than what we will encounter in the texts we will look at. I want you to think about why.

We are talking about the temperance movement beginning before the mid 19th and spanning to the early 20th century. This was a massive set of campaigns aimed at getting people to drink less or to not drink at all.

It is important to understand a few of things about this period. One was industrialization—the growth of railroads and major industries in the US. Another was general sense among people in America that as the country developed, society could be improved. There was also religious revival—you will see a reference in your reading to the Second Great Awakening. This was a wave of increased interest in devotion to Christian faith, particularly among Protestants. This term generally refers to groups that split from the Catholic Church in Europe in the 1500s. If you drive around most cities or towns in America today, you will see several churches—a few will be Orthodox or Catholic, but most will be Protestant. You will also see the term evangelicals. This usually refers to Protestants who place strong emphasis on the importance and accuracy of the Bible and on bringing other people to join their faith. So, as interest in Protestant Christianity grew, more people felt that religious moral convictions could be used to address society's problems.

A major problem was alcohol consumption—in the early 1800s, Americans consumed three times as much alcohol, per capita, as they do today. And there were consequences. Many men spent their earnings at saloons, taking needed resources from their families. And while far fewer women worked outside of the home, there were growing women's rights movements. Many of the same women working for women's suffrage, the right to vote, also supported temperance. In fact, one notably strong organization was the WCTU (Women's Christian Temperance Union). While other movements for the improvement of society made important differences, none were as large or had as great of impact as the temperance movement.
